2 Litre Bottles: the Handle, the Base and the Process Decide

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 11 November 2026

At 2 litres the pack weighs over two kilograms full. The handle becomes a mould feature, the base becomes a structural part, and the process is no longer a choice between equals.

Two litres is the point where a bottle stops being a bottle and becomes a container. It is sold full of detergent, oil, vinegar, water or bleach, it weighs well over two kilograms when filled, and it is lifted by a handle that has to be designed into the pack rather than added to it afterwards.

Almost every problem at this size traces to one of three decisions: the process, the handle, or the base. The decoration and the label are the last things that matter, which is the opposite of how a 100 ml bottle is specified.

Why the Process Decides Everything at 2 L

A 2 L HDPE bottle for detergent or bleach is extrusion blow moulded: a tube of hot resin is inflated inside a two-part mould, the handle is formed by pinching that tube, and the wall thickness is set by the parison programme rather than by a core. It is the cheapest way to make an opaque container, and it tolerates the recycled HDPE that most of this category now uses.

A clear 2 L PET bottle for juice or water is injection blow moulded: a preform is injection moulded first, then stretched and blown. The stretch orients the molecules in two directions, which is why a 2 L PET bottle holds its shape at 0.35 mm wall while the HDPE equivalent needs 0.8 mm to stand up on a shelf.

The two are not interchangeable. Extrusion blow cannot make clear PET, and injection blow is not economic for the thick, handled wall that a detergent pack wants. There is a third route in some briefs — moulding the body in two halves and welding — and at 2 L it is the wrong answer: the weld sits exactly where the pack carries its own weight.

The Handle Is a Tool, Not an Accessory

An integrated handle needs the mould to pinch the parison around a handle bridge, so the cavity has a shaped insert and the shot produces a scrap slug that has to be trimmed. It adds to the tool, it adds a trimming step, and it constrains the body shape: the handle has to sit where the parison can reach it, which is the back of the bottle and never the shoulder.

The handle bore is a dimension, not a guess. 25–30 mm across fits an adult hand with a glove; 20 mm is a hook that most buyers reject on the second order. The handle also sets the balance. A full 2 L pack weighs 2.1–2.2 kg, and a handle placed high makes it tip forward as it empties; the centre of the handle should sit at or below the centre of gravity of the full pack.

Where the shape cannot take an integrated handle, the alternative is a clip-on handle or one moulded into the closure. Both cost less in tooling and more per unit, and both are worth considering when the bottle has to nest empty: a handled bottle that stacks ships in a smaller carton than one that does not.

Base Sag Is the Failure That Shows up in the Warehouse

An empty 2 L bottle is stiff. A full one is a two-kilogram column of liquid standing on a base that may be 0.8 mm thick at the centre. Under a pallet stack the base creeps, the bottle leans, and by the time the container is unpacked the pack rocks instead of standing.

The fix is geometric, not material. A push-up dome under the base with a defined foot ring puts the load on a circle rather than on a flat sheet, and the dome gives the resin somewhere to move without dragging the foot. The foot ring has to span the carton grid, and the base radius has to be generous enough that the mould can be polished.

Test it before approving: fill to nominal, cap, stand the bottle at 40 °C for 72 hours under the weight of a full pallet, then measure the height and check for rocker. A pass at room temperature in the sample room proves nothing. Hot-fill products fail here first, because the fill temperature softens the base before the resin has had time to settle.

Necks at 2 L: Faster Filling, Harder Pouring

38 mm is the default for detergent and refill packs. It fills fast, it pours a thick liquid, and it takes a measuring cap. 28/410 is chosen when the pack has to take a trigger or a pump from an existing range, which is why 2 L cleaner refills often keep the small neck and accept slower filling.

A wide neck on a large bottle glugs. Air has to get in while the liquid gets out, and at 2 L the vacuum is strong enough to collapse a soft body or to splash. A vented cap, or a small air hole beside the spout, is cheaper than a wider neck and fixes the pour rather than the filling line.

43 mm and above belongs to viscous products — fabric softener, sauces, gels. The cap becomes a measuring device and sometimes a dosing chamber, and at that point the closure costs more than the bottle.

What It Costs, and Where the MOQ Lands

Tooling for a 2 L pack sits above a 500 ml pack for two reasons: the mould is physically larger, and a handled body needs a side action. The large injection-blow band in our published benchmarks runs USD 4,700–9,500 with an MOQ of 10,000–20,000 pcs, because a tool that size has to be paid for by volume. An extrusion blow tool for an opaque handled bottle is cheaper per cavity, and its MOQ lands at 3,000–5,000 pcs.

That gap is the whole economics of the size. A clear 2 L PET bottle is a volume product with a big tool and a high minimum. An opaque HDPE 2 L is a smaller tool and a lower minimum, which is why a new brand launching a 2 L refill usually starts in HDPE and moves to PET only when the volume justifies the tool. These are ranges from our own quotations, not a quote for your pack.

Freight is the second cost that surprises buyers. Empty bottles nest and ship cheaply. Filled, a 2 L pack is 2.1 kg, and a 20 ft container reaches its weight limit at roughly half the unit count it could carry in 500 ml bottles. If the product ships filled, ocean freight per unit can exceed the cost of the bottle.

When 2 Litres Is the Wrong Size

A concentrate in a 2 L pack is a different product from a ready-to-use 2 L, and the pack has to say so: a dosing cap, a dilution instruction on the label, and a neck that pours reliably at small volumes. If the buyer decants, the handle matters less than the cap.

Above 2.5 L the pack becomes a jug with a different base geometry and a different pallet pattern, and above 5 L it is a drum. Each step changes the process and the tool. A 3 L brief is not a stretched 2 L — if the volume is 3 L or 4 L, say so at the first enquiry rather than after the drawing.

Questions We Get on This

What is a 2 litre bottle made of?

HDPE for detergent, bleach and refill packs; PET for clear juice, oil and water. PP appears where the product is filled hot.

Why do 2 L bottles have handles?

A full pack weighs over 2 kg. The handle is formed in the mould or clipped on, and its position sets whether the pack balances as it empties.

Can a clear 2 L PET bottle have an integral handle?

Rarely. A clear 2 L PET body is injection blow moulded, and the handle is normally a separate clip-on part, because the process cannot form an integral handle at a sensible wall thickness.

What is the MOQ for a 2 L bottle?

3,000–5,000 pcs on an extrusion blow tool; 10,000–20,000 pcs on a large injection blow tool. Indicated ranges, not a quote.

Why does my 2 L bottle rock instead of standing?

Base creep under a pallet stack. A push-up dome and a wider foot ring are the fix, verified at 40 °C for 72 hours rather than at room temperature.
