rPET Jars: Colour, Wall Thickness and the Declaration

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 12 November 2026

A jar is injection moulded, so recycled PET shows up differently than in a bottle: the tint sits in a 2–3 mm wall, the documentation is per batch, and the percentage on the artwork is the least reliable part of the claim.

Recycled PET in a jar is a different problem from recycled PET in a bottle. A bottle is blow moulded at 0.4–0.8 mm wall and the resin is stretched, which hides a lot. A jar is injection moulded at 1.5–3 mm, the wall is looked through rather than past, and every batch-to-batch colour shift is visible on a shelf under a shop light.

That is why a jar brief with a recycled-content target comes down to three questions, in this order: what percentage, what colour tolerance, and which declaration can be issued with the shipment. The grade and the price follow from those, not the other way round.

Why a Jar Is Not a Bottle

A bottle is a blown shape: the resin is stretched thin and the wall is a membrane. A jar is an injection moulding: the wall is a structural panel 1.5–3 mm thick, the base is solid, and the resin has to flow the whole height of the cavity without a weld line showing on a transparent face.

The material itself is covered on the rPET material page; this page is about what it does to a jar.

That changes the resin requirement. Injection grade rPET sits at a lower intrinsic viscosity than bottle grade, because it has to flow a thick section; and it has to be dried hard — moisture above roughly 50 ppm hydrolyses the chain and the jar goes brittle at the base corner. A moulder who runs bottle-grade rPET in a jar tool gets flow lines; one who skips the dryer gets cracking.

It also changes what the customer sees. In a 0.5 mm bottle wall the recycled tint reads as a colour; in a 2.5 mm jar wall it reads as depth, and the eye compares it against the cap and the label. That is why colour approval on a recycled jar takes a sample round rather than a Pantone number.

Three Documents, Not One

The first document is the resin declaration: which grade, what percentage by weight, which recycler. It is issued per resin lot, not per order, so a 12-month programme needs a lot trace.

The second is the process authorisation for food contact. In the EU, recycled plastic for food contact falls under Regulation (EU) 2022/1616 on recycled plastic materials, which requires an authorised recycling process and a declaration of compliance for the finished article on top of the EU 10/2011 migration testing. For the US, the equivalent is an FDA no-objection letter for the recycling process. A supplier who cannot name the process cannot support a food-contact claim, whatever the percentage says.

The third is the batch declaration that travels with the shipment: lot number, percentage, declaration of compliance reference. This is the one an auditor asks for, and the one that is most often missing because it is issued at the end of the run rather than the start.

One more distinction worth writing into the purchase order: physical segregation versus mass balance. Physically segregated recycled content means the jar in your hand contains that percentage. Mass balance means the recycler bought that much recycled feedstock across a period and attributes it to a production run. Both can be legal; they are not the same claim, and a brand that publishes a percentage should know which one it is buying.

The Colour Question

At 30 % the tint is close to virgin PET: slightly warm, slightly less bright, and most buyers accept it against a known sample. At 50 % a grey-green cast appears and recyclers add a light blue toner to neutralise the yellowness — that is why so many recycled jars look faintly blue rather than green.

Above 70 % the cast stops being a tint and becomes a colour. Clear rPET at that level is not water-clear and will not match a virgin sample; the honest options are to raise the wall and call it frosted, to go opaque, or to accept the colour as the pack's identity.

Opaque is the easy answer. A white, black or coloured jar hides the batch variation entirely, and the recycled content becomes a documentation claim rather than a visual one. If the brand needs a specific Pantone in a transparent jar, 30 % is the practical ceiling for a first approval.

Wall Thickness and the 2–3 mm Problem

Recycled PET flows slightly differently from virgin and cools slightly differently. In a thin wall that does not matter; in a 3 mm jar it shows up as sink marks opposite the gate, or as a base that is not flat because the core and cavity cooled at different rates.

The practical fixes are process, not design: a slower injection profile, a hotter mould on the core side, and a longer hold. All three add cycle time — typically 10–20 % on a jar, which is why a recycled jar can cost more than the virgin version even when the resin price is the same.

If the jar has a thick base with a logo moulded into it, expect the first sample round to be about the base. That is also where rPET and virgin diverge most visibly, because the base is the thickest section and the slowest to cool.

What It Costs, and When It Makes Sense

Recycled PET is not reliably cheaper than virgin. The price moves with the collection market, and there are periods when rPET carries a premium of 10–20 %. The reason to use it is a regulatory or brand commitment, not a saving.

The commitments are real: recycled-content targets for packaging are phasing in across the EU under the PPWR, and several brand owners now publish percentages per pack. A jar is often where a brand can hit a target most cheaply, because it is a rigid, single-material part with no barrier layer to complicate recycling.

The MOQ moves with the resin, not the mould. A stock jar tool needs 1,000 pcs; a recycled-content run usually starts at 3,000–5,000 pcs because the resin is bought in tonne lots and a smaller run leaves the moulder holding the balance. Ask for the resin lot size before fixing the order quantity.

What to Send in a Brief

Percentage, colour expectation, food-contact market, and order quantity. Those four decide the grade, the declaration and whether a stock tool can be used.

Send a photo or a physical sample of the colour you are matching, and say whether mass balance is acceptable. Both answers change the price, and neither can be inferred from the words recycled PET.

Questions We Get on This

Are rPET jars food safe?

Yes, if the recycler's process is authorised and the finished jar has a declaration of compliance under EU 2022/1616 plus EU 10/2011 migration testing — or an FDA no-objection letter for the US. The percentage alone proves nothing.

Why do recycled jars look slightly blue?

Recyclers add a light blue toner to neutralise the yellow cast of recycled PET. Above about 50 % the tint becomes visible; at 70 % and above it reads as a colour rather than a tint.

Can I get a clear rPET jar that matches virgin?

Not exactly. At 30 % it is close; above that the cast is visible. For an exact Pantone, use an opaque colour where the batch variation is hidden.

What is the MOQ for an rPET jar?

1,000 pcs on a stock mould, but 3,000–5,000 pcs is typical for a recycled-content run because the resin is bought in tonne lots. Ranges, not a quote.

Does rPET cost more than virgin PET?

Sometimes. The premium has run 10–20 % in tight markets; at other times it is at parity. It is a compliance decision, not a saving.

Can the same jar mould run virgin and recycled?

Yes, with a process change: recycled PET needs harder drying and a slower injection profile. The tool is shared; the parameters are not.
