Bottle Mould Maintenance: What a Tool Needs Between Runs

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 11 November 2026

A mould has a service life measured in shots, a wear pattern that starts at the neck, and an owner. Most tooling failures are storage and record failures, not design failures.

A bottle mould is not a consumable and not a permanent asset. It is tooling with a finite life, a predictable wear pattern and a maintenance schedule that usually gets written down only after the first bad batch.

Most of the tooling problems we are asked to diagnose are not design failures. They are a neck insert that was never checked, a tool stored on its side for two years, or a record that cannot say how many shots the mould has run. This is what the maintenance actually consists of, and what it costs when it is skipped.

What Wears First Is Not the Body

The body of a bottle mould carries the shape and sees relatively little abrasion. The neck insert carries a thread and a sealing land and takes a cap on and off for the life of the tool, which is thousands of small rubs against a hard edge. That is why the neck is the first dimension to move.

On an extrusion blow mould the neck is usually a separate steel insert in an aluminium body. The insert is replaceable, and replacing it is a fraction of the cost of a new tool. A buyer who is told the whole mould must be replaced usually has a supplier who did not design the neck as an insert.

The second wear point is the pinch-off — the edge that welds and cuts the parison tail on an extrusion blow tool. When it rounds, the tail does not cut cleanly, and the flash that used to be trimmed in one pass needs a second operation. The symptom is a trimming cost that creeps up without anything visible changing on the bottle.

Injection moulds wear differently: the cavity gate, the ejector marks and the cooling near the gate. On a two-cavity bottle tool the two cavities rarely wear at the same rate, which is why weight drift between cavities is a better wear indicator than absolute weight.

How Many Shots a Tool Really Lasts

An aluminium blow mould typically runs into the hundreds of thousands of shots. A steel injection tool runs into the millions. Both numbers move by a factor of two or more with the resin: a mineral-filled or glass-filled compound is abrasive, a soft HDPE with a good pigment is not.

Cooling water matters as much as the resin. Hard water scales the channels, scale insulates, the tool runs hot, the cycle lengthens, and the operator compensates by lowering the water temperature or speeding the cycle. Both put more stress on the tool, and the tool is what fails.

Cavitation changes the maths. A two-cavity tool produces twice the parts per shot but wears twice as fast in absolute shots, so the service interval in calendar time does not change — but the record has to be kept per cavity, not per tool.

Between Runs: the Part That Is Usually Skipped

Clean the cavity with a solvent that suits the resin, not with a general-purpose degreaser that leaves a film. Dry it, because water in a closed mould is how a polished cavity gets a rust bloom between runs. Oil the cavity faces lightly, and close the tool before it goes into storage.

Store it upright on a pallet, never stacked, never on its side. A mould that stands on its side rests on the parting line, and the parting line is the surface that has to be perfect. Block the water ports so nothing crawls in, and write the shot count on the tool or in the record before it goes into the rack.

Before every production run, check the neck insert against the drawing — go/no-go gauge, not a caliper on the thread — and check the parting line for dents. Two minutes at the start of a run is the cheapest maintenance in the factory, and it is the step that gets skipped when the schedule is tight.

The Symptoms That Say the Tool Is Due

Flash at the parting line is the first and the most visible. It means the two halves are no longer clamping cleanly, either from a dent, from resin build-up or from worn alignment. Polishing fixes a build-up; a dent has to be welded and re-cut.

Weight drift between cavities of more than about 2–3 % is the second. It points at a cavity that is no longer filling the same way, usually because the gate or the cooling has changed. Left alone, it becomes a dimensional reject on the neck rather than the body.

Neck ovality shows up before it is measured: caps start to cam off, torque readings scatter, and the leak test fails on a bottle that looks perfect. By the time it is visible on a gauge the insert has usually been out of specification for a while.

A cycle time that creeps up with no change in the recipe is a cooling problem, not a machine problem. Check the channels before adjusting the process.

Repair Is Usually an Insert, Not a Tool

A worn neck insert is replaced, not repaired. New inserts can be cut in two to three weeks, and the cost is a small fraction of a new mould. The body keeps its shape because the shape is held by the cavity, which is not what wore out.

A damaged parting line is welded and re-polished, then spotted back to the other half. That is a specialist job and it is worth doing: a re-spotted parting line can hold a seal for another few hundred thousand shots.

A body that can no longer hold its dimensions — usually after a bad repair or after being run outside its specified tonnage — is beyond economic repair. At that point the honest answer is a new tool, and the honest question is whether the volume still justifies it.

Who Owns the Mould, and What Happens When You Move

The customer who paid for the tool owns it. That is the position we take in writing before the first payment, and it means the tool can be shipped, scrapped or moved to another moulder on instruction. It also means the customer decides whether it is serviced.

Ownership is only half of it. A mould is useless without its drawings, its process sheet and its shot record, so the handover pack matters as much as the steel: cavity layout, neck insert specification, cooling circuit diagram, the resin grades the tool has run, and any repairs. Ask for that pack before you pay, not after you decide to leave.

We store moulds for customers between runs — 3,000+ tools in the racks — and the ones that come back into production cleanly are the ones with a record. A tool that arrives with no history has to be inspected, measured and treated as new before it is run, which costs more than the storage it saved.

Questions We Get on This

How long does a bottle mould last?

An aluminium blow mould typically runs into the hundreds of thousands of shots; a steel injection tool runs into the millions. Abrasive pigments and scaled cooling channels shorten both.

Who owns the mould?

The customer who paid for it. It can be shipped or scrapped on instruction, and the drawings and process sheet should travel with it.

How often should a mould be serviced?

Check the neck insert and the parting line before every run. A full service — polish, descale the channels, re-spot the parting line — is a rule of thumb every 100,000–200,000 shots, or annually on a tool that runs occasionally.

What is the first sign of wear?

Flash at the parting line. Then weight drift between cavities. Neck ovality shows up as scattered cap torque before it shows on a gauge.

Can a worn mould be repaired?

Usually. Neck and cavity inserts are replaceable at a fraction of a new tool, and a damaged parting line can be welded and re-polished. A body that can no longer hold its dimensions is the exception.
