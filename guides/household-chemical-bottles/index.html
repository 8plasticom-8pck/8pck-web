Household Chemical Bottles: What Each Product Demands

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 26 October 2026

Bleach, drain cleaner, floor cleaner and dish soap look like the same bottle. The chemistry decides the resin, the vent, the closure and the label — and getting it wrong fails after two months on a shelf.

Household chemicals are the least glamorous part of packaging and the most likely to fail. The reason is that the bottle is chosen for cost and the chemistry is chosen for performance, and nobody tests the pair before the first container.

This page covers the eight products that make up most of the category, and the three failure modes that repeat: resin stress-cracking, closure corrosion, and liner creep.

Bleach and Caustic: the Two That Cannot Use PET

Sodium hypochlorite attacks PET. A bleach bottle in PET will stress-crack at the shoulder within weeks, usually along the moulding line, and the leak appears at the worst possible moment — in a warehouse, on a pallet, over other goods.

HDPE is the only sensible resin for bleach and for caustic drain cleaner. The second requirement is the closure: a standard lined cap traps pressure as the hypochlorite slowly decomposes, so bleach bottles use a vented cap or a liner with a venting slit. A sealed, unvented cap on bleach is a swelling bottle waiting to happen.

Child-Resistant Is Not Optional for Caustic

In the EU, the US and most export markets, drain cleaner and anything above a defined caustic concentration requires a child-resistant closure. That is a legal requirement, not a premium feature, and it changes the neck: a CR cap needs a specific thread and often a collar, so the bottle cannot be switched to a standard cap later without a new tool.

The practical consequence is that CR bottles should be specified with the closure from the start. Retrofitting a CR collar to a bottle designed for a screw cap usually means a second mould.

Trigger and Spray Products: the Head Decides

Glass cleaner, oven cleaner and car shampoo often ship with a trigger. The trigger's spring is the weak point in ammonia and in alkaline foam formulas, and the nozzle clogs with any product that has a suspended solid.

For oven cleaner specifically, a foam trigger with a wide orifice is the only type that works: a fine mist head will clog within the first use on a gel. For glass cleaner, a fine mist is correct and the 0.2 ml output is what the user expects on a window.

Liners, Creep and the Slow Leak

Fabric softener is the classic liner failure: the cationic surfactant creeps through an EPE foam liner over months, and the bottle arrives with a greasy film on the outside. The fix is a barrier liner or an induction seal, not a tighter cap.

The same applies to any product with a high surfactant load, including dish soap at the concentrated end. A liner that passes a 24-hour leak test can still fail a three-month shelf test, which is why chemical packs should be tested on the shelf, not on the bench.

Recycled Content in Chemical Packs

PCR HDPE is now routine for household chemicals, because the colour is usually opaque and the resin does not need food-contact approval. 30–50 % PCR HDPE in a bleach or floor cleaner bottle is a normal specification today.

The constraint is the same as elsewhere: recycled HDPE varies batch to batch, so a white bottle needs a masterbatch plan, and the wall may need to go from 0.55 to 0.60 mm at the base to keep impact strength.

Sizes That Actually Sell, and Why

Dish soap and glass cleaner live at 500–750 ml because the product is used at the sink and refilled often. Floor cleaner and fabric softener move to 1–2 L because the dose is per bucket or per wash and the buyer wants fewer trips.

Bleach sits at 750 ml to 2 L depending on market: UK and US households buy 2 L, continental Europe and Asia more often 750 ml–1 L. That difference is worth knowing before a tool is cut, because the same shape at 1 L and 2 L is two moulds, not one.

Labels That Survive the Chemistry

A paper label on a bleach bottle will bleach. The label for any hypochlorite or caustic product has to be a PP or PET film, and the adhesive has to be solvent-resistant, or the label lifts at the corner within a month in a humid warehouse.

The same applies to the print on a trigger: solvent-based inks on a PP trigger can be wiped off by the product itself when the user's hands are wet with it. For chemical packs, the label and the ink are part of the compatibility test, not decoration.

Packing and Freight for Heavy Liquids

Household chemicals are heavy: a 2 L floor cleaner at 2.1 kg fills a container fast, and the carton count is set by weight rather than volume. That is why 2 L packs often ship in 6s rather than 12s, and why the pallet pattern changes between 1 L and 2 L.

For export, an induction seal plus a shrink band is the usual combination on screw caps; trigger packs ship with heads loose below about 20,000 units, because a fitted trigger adds height to every carton.

Questions We Get on This

Can I put bleach in a PET bottle?

No. Sodium hypochlorite stress-cracks PET within weeks. Bleach requires HDPE, and a vented closure.

Do drain cleaner bottles need child-resistant caps?

Yes in the EU, US and most export markets. It is a legal requirement for caustic products, and it changes the neck specification.

Why does my fabric softener bottle get greasy?

The cationic surfactant creeps through a standard EPE liner. Use a barrier liner or an induction seal, and test on the shelf rather than over 24 hours.

What trigger fits a 500 ml glass cleaner bottle?

A 28/410 fine mist trigger at 0.15–0.25 ml per stroke. The bottle neck is standard; the trigger's spring is what decides whether it survives ammonia.

Can household chemical bottles use recycled plastic?

Yes. 30–50 % PCR HDPE is routine because the packs are opaque. The wall at the base may need 0.05 mm more to hold impact strength.
