Bottle Cap Types: Disc-Top, Flip-Top, Screw and Pump

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 26 October 2026

Four closures cover most bottles, and the choice is decided by viscosity, one-handed use and whether the pack will leak in a bag.

A bottle and a cap are quoted together but chosen separately. The bottle is decided by volume and shape; the cap is decided by the product inside — how thick it is, whether it will be used with wet hands, and whether the pack travels in a bag.

Four closures cover most of the market: disc-top, flip-top, screw with a liner, and the pump. Each has a cost index and a different failure mode, and the failure mode is what the customer complains about.

Disc-Top: the Default for Anything Squeezed

A disc-top opens by pressing one side, so it works one-handed with a wet hand — which is why shampoo and body wash live in it. The orifice decides the flow: 3 mm for lotion, 5–6 mm for shampoo, 8 mm for thick gel.

Its weak point is the hinge and the lid seal. A disc-top left open in a shower will collect water, and a thin liquid will find the gap. For a travel pack, a flip-top with a tighter lid, or a screw cap, is the safer choice.

Flip-Top: Cheap, Fast, and the Hinge Is the Whole Story

A flip-top is the cheapest closure that stays attached to the bottle, which matters on a filling line: no separate cap to orient, no liner to drop. The hinge is a living hinge — a thin PP bridge that flexes for the life of the pack.

The hinge fails in two ways: it snaps after a few thousand openings, or it creeps open when the bottle is squeezed hard. Oils are the worst case, because the residue creeps into the hinge and holds it open.

Screw Cap With a Liner: the Export Default

A screw cap plus an induction or EPE liner is the lowest-cost closure that survives a sea container without leaking. It is also the only closure that is genuinely tamper-evident when the liner is induction-sealed.

The cost of that reliability is user experience: it needs two hands, and it is the closure most often left off. For food, chemicals and anything shipped by LCL, it is still the right answer.

Pump and Sprayer: the Expensive Closures

A lotion pump costs two to three times a disc-top, and a fine mist sprayer more still. The money is in the engine: the spring, the ball valve and the dip tube have to work for the number of strokes the pack promises.

The most common mistake is matching a pump to a bottle without testing the pair. A 24/410 pump on a bottle moulded to the loose end of the neck tolerance will draw air, and the customer will report a pump that 'does not work' when the bottle is the problem.

For air-sensitive formulas, a standard pump is the wrong answer: it pulls air back into the bottle on every stroke. That is what airless packs exist for.

Liner, Seal and the Two Failures That Cost Money

Inside a screw cap there is a liner — EPE foam, a PE disc, or an induction foil. The liner is what actually seals; the cap only holds it in compression. Switch liners without telling the customer and the leak rate changes, even though the cap looks identical.

The first failure is a liner that is too hard: it seals at the factory and leaks after a lorry ride, because the plastic relaxes and compression drops. The second is a liner that is too soft: it deforms into the thread and the cap backs off. Both are solved by testing the assembled pack, not the components.

Child-Resistant and Tamper-Evident, Without Overpaying

A child-resistant closure is a two-motion cap: squeeze and turn, or push down and turn. It costs more than a standard cap and it slows the filling line, because each cap has to be oriented and torqued rather than pressed on.

Tamper evidence is cheaper to buy than child resistance, and it comes in three forms: an induction seal inside the cap, a drop-ring that breaks on first opening, and a shrink band. For export to the EU and US, a broken ring plus an induction seal is the usual combination; a shrink band is the cheapest option and the most often cut off by hand.

The Neck Thread, Briefly — and Why 24/410 Keeps Winning

A thread is defined by diameter and profile: 24/410 is a 24 mm neck with a 410 profile. The 410 profile is the short, coarse thread that has become the default for personal care worldwide, which is why so many closures are available off the shelf in it.

28/410 exists for the same reason at a larger diameter: bigger orifice, bigger pump, more torque. A 28/410 pump moves roughly 1.5–2 ml per stroke against 1.0–1.3 ml for 24/410, which is why shampoo and body wash move up to it as the pack gets bigger.

Anything more exotic — 20/410, 18/415, snap-on — narrows the choice of closures to what one factory happens to mould. The thread is not a place to be original; the shoulder and the label are.

What to Specify, in Order

Start with the product: viscosity, whether it is oil or water based, and whether it must stay sterile. Then the use: one hand or two, in a shower or on a shelf, travel or bathroom. Then the logistics: does it ship by LCL, does it need a tamper-evident seal, does the retailer require a child-resistant closure.

Only then pick the neck. The neck is the consequence of those three answers, not the starting point — and it is the number that will be hardest to change once the bottle tool exists.

Questions We Get on This

What is the most common bottle cap?

A disc-top on 24/410 for personal care, and a screw cap with an EPE liner for food and chemicals. Between them they cover most of the market.

Can I change the cap later without changing the bottle?

Yes, if the neck stays the same. A 24/410 bottle takes a disc-top, a flip-top, a screw cap or a lotion pump without touching the mould.

Which cap leaks least in transit?

A screw cap with an induction seal. Pumps and disc-tops are tested on the bottle before packing, but a sealed screw cap is the only closure designed to survive a container.

What does 24/410 mean on a cap?

24 mm is the neck diameter, 410 is the thread profile. Any cap marked 24/410 fits any bottle with a 24/410 neck.

Are child-resistant caps available?

Yes, on 18/410 for small volumes and 24/410 for larger ones. They are standard for essential oils, CBD and any product with a toxicity risk.
