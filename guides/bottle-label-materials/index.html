Bottle Label Materials: Shower, Fridge, and the Recycling Line

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 11 November 2026

Four materials cover almost every bottle label, and the choice is decided by where the pack is used, how the label is applied, and what the recycling stream does with it.

A label is the cheapest part of a bottle and the only part that carries the mandatory text. That disproportion is why it gets specified last, and why most complaints about a finished pack are about a label that peeled, wrinkled or would not come off.

Four material families cover almost every bottle: paper, PP film, PET film and shrink sleeve. The choice is not about quality. It is three questions — where the pack is used, how the label is applied, and what happens to it when the bottle is empty.

Four Families, One Job

Paper is the oldest and still the cheapest face stock. In wet-glue form it is applied with a starch adhesive to glass and to large PET bottles; in self-adhesive form it is bought on a roll and applied by machine. It prints beautifully and it is the only family that is genuinely cheap at low volume.

PP film is the workhorse for anything that gets wet. It is white or clear, it takes print well, and it does not care about a bathroom. PET film is the premium version for clear-on-clear work: a clear PET label on a clear PET bottle disappears, which is how a 250 ml shampoo gets a floating logo with no visible carrier.

Shrink sleeve is not a label in the same sense. It is a tube of film shrunk around the whole body, so it covers 360° and can wrap a shoulder or a curved base. It also hides the bottle, which is a design decision as much as a printing one.

Direct decoration — silkscreen, hot stamp, spray coat — sits outside this list, because it is printed onto the bottle rather than onto a carrier. It is the right answer when the label has to disappear or when the volume is high enough that no carrier makes sense.

The Bathroom Test

Water is the first filter. An uncoated paper label on a shampoo bottle lifts at the edges within days: the fibre wicks moisture, the adhesive is undermined, and the label curls from the corner where the hand grabs it. A laminated or varnished paper face stock survives far better, but the cut edge is still an entry point.

PP film is the default for anything used in a shower or a bathroom. It does not absorb water, it does not swell, and it holds its shape against condensation on a cold bottle. Where the budget allows only one upgrade on a wet product, it is the label material, not the adhesive.

Oil is the second filter and it is less obvious. A body oil or a massage oil wets the label from the outside every time the bottle is handled, and an oil-soaked paper label turns translucent in a way that looks like a manufacturing defect. PP handles it; paper needs a film laminate, at which point the paper is only there for the print.

Adhesive Is the Half Nobody Specifies

The adhesive decides whether the label stays on the shelf for two years and whether it comes off in the recycling wash. Permanent is the default; removable is for refill packs and for anything the customer is meant to peel; freezer grade stays tacky at −18 °C where a standard adhesive turns brittle and lets go.

Surface energy is where labels fail quietly. HDPE and LDPE have low surface energy — around 31 mN/m — so an adhesive that grips glass will slide on a soft squeeze bottle, especially on the curved shoulder where the label is under tension. The test is not a thumb; it is a 24-hour dwell at room temperature, then a peel at the angle the label will actually see.

Ask for the adhesive by name and by grade, not by the word permanent. Two permanent adhesives can differ by a factor of two in peel strength, and the difference only shows on the second pallet in a hot warehouse.

Shrink Sleeve: 360 Degrees and a Recycling Catch

A sleeve needs a body that is close to straight. It can follow a gentle taper and a soft shoulder, but a sharp shoulder, a heavy grip panel or a handle will leave a bridge of film that never touches the bottle. That bridge is where a sleeve tears, and where the print distorts.

The sleeve has to be perforated. Perforations let the consumer open the pack without a knife and let the sleeve tear away from the cap. They also let air out during shrinking, which is why a sleeve without perforation usually ends up with a bubble at the shoulder.

The catch is material. A PETG sleeve on a PET bottle is the worst combination for a recycling line: the two plastics have similar density, so the sleeve does not separate from the flake, and the recycled PET comes out contaminated. A PP sleeve floats and separates, at the cost of a slightly duller finish and a higher shrink temperature.

Sleeves also cost more per unit than a label at low volume, because the film is printed on a wide web and the minimum run is set in metres rather than in pieces. Below about 10,000 units a wrap-around label is almost always the better buy.

What the Recycling Stream Does With Each

Every EU market is moving the same way: the label is part of the pack, and the pack has to be recyclable as a unit. In practice that means the label must either separate cleanly in the wash, or be made of the same polymer as the bottle.

PP labels float. In a sink-float tank — the standard first step in PET recycling — the PP label and the HDPE cap come off the top while the PET sinks, which is why a PP label on a PET bottle is accepted in most schemes. Paper labels are accepted where a wash-off adhesive is used and the fibre can be filtered out; with a hot-melt permanent adhesive they are a contaminant.

The direction of travel is visible in the packaging regulation now working through the EU: design for recycling, and the label is in scope. A sleeve that hides 100 % of the bottle is a design choice that may cost a fee in some markets, and a sleeve that is the same polymer as the bottle is the version that survives.

What to Send With the Artwork

Label size with 3 mm bleed, the corner radius, and the height on the bottle where it sits. Then the production detail: unwind direction, core diameter, roll direction and maximum roll diameter, because a roll that is wound the wrong way stops the applicator rather than the press.

Send one bottle from the actual mould, not a render. Labels are applied around a curve, and a 60 mm label that fits a flat proof will gap at the seam on a 63 mm body. We apply in house from 1,000 pcs with a three-day addition to the lead time, and we ask for a roll sample so the applicator is set before the run rather than during it.

Questions We Get on This

What is the most waterproof bottle label?

PP film. It is the default for shampoo, body wash and anything used in a shower, and it does not need a laminate.

Can paper labels go on a shampoo bottle?

Only with a coated or laminated face stock, and even then the cut edge is a weak point. For a wet product PP is the cheaper answer over the life of the pack.

Is a shrink sleeve recyclable?

The bottle may be; the sleeve often is not. A PETG sleeve on a PET bottle contaminates the flake, while a PP sleeve separates by flotation.

Which adhesive for a freezer product?

A freezer-grade permanent adhesive. Standard adhesives turn brittle at −18 °C and let go at the edges.

How many labels do I need to order?

Printing usually starts at 5,000–10,000 pcs. We apply from 1,000 pcs, adding about three days to the lead time.

Can you apply labels we print ourselves?

Yes, roll-fed. Send a roll sample so the applicator is set before the production run.
