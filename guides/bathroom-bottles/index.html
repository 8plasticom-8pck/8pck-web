Bathroom Bottles: Mouthwash, Micellar Water and the Rest

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 26 October 2026

Mouthwash, micellar water, makeup remover, contact lens solution, nail polish remover, self-tanner and sunscreen share a shelf and almost nothing else. Each one breaks a different part of the pack.

The bathroom shelf is the most chemically diverse place in the house: a mouthwash with 20 % alcohol, an acetone remover, a bi-phase makeup remover with oil, and a sunscreen with UV filters in an emulsion. They are sold in similar bottles and every one of them fails for a different reason.

Acetone: the One That Eats PET

Nail polish remover is mostly acetone or ethyl acetate, and both dissolve PET. A remover in a PET bottle will soften the shoulder and eventually craze; the pack has to be HDPE or glass, with a liner that resists ketones.

The label has the same problem: most pressure-sensitive adhesives are attacked by acetone, so the label lifts at the edge. A PP film with a solvent-resistant adhesive, or direct print, is the only reliable option.

Alcohol and the Mouthwash Haze

Mouthwash at 15–25 % ethanol is fine in PET, but a thin wall plus a hot warehouse can produce a slight haze at the shoulder over months. The fix is wall thickness rather than a different resin: 0.55 mm hazed, 0.7 mm did not, in our own sample runs.

The closure matters more than the bottle. An induction seal is the standard for a liquid that ships on its side, and a simple screw cap with an EPE liner will weep alcohol over a long sea freight.

Sunscreen and the Emulsion Problem

Sunscreen is an emulsion with UV filters, and the filters are the aggressive part: avobenzone and octocrylene attack some gaskets and soften some printing inks. A pump or disc-top with a silicone or PE seal survives; a cheap foam gasket swells.

The second issue is the pack's shape in use: sunscreen is squeezed with wet, sandy hands, so the bottle needs a waist or a grip and the closure needs a large thumb tab. A smooth cylinder with a small flip-top is the wrong pack for a beach bag.

Sterility: Contact Lens Solution Is a Different Category

Contact lens solution is not a chemistry problem, it is a sterility problem. The bottle has to keep the contents sterile through a nozzle that the user touches to their eye, and the nozzle design — not the resin — decides whether it does.

That is why lens solution bottles are almost always HDPE or a multi-layer structure with a one-way nozzle, and why they are filled in a controlled area. For a brand entering this category, the pack is the easy part and the filling environment is the barrier.

Sizes and the Shelf

Mouthwash is 250–500 ml, micellar water 200–400 ml, sunscreen 50–200 ml, remover 50–200 ml. These are shelf-driven, not formula-driven: the retailer allocates a facing and the bottle has to fill it without looking empty.

The consequence for tooling is that a single shape is usually cut in two or three sizes with the same neck, so the closures and the label design carry across. Cutting one size and hoping is how a range ends up with three unrelated bottles.

Questions We Get on This

What bottle is used for nail polish remover?

HDPE or glass with a ketone-resistant liner. Acetone dissolves PET, so a PET remover bottle will fail within months.

Can mouthwash go in a thin PET bottle?

It can, but a 0.55 mm wall can haze over months in a hot warehouse. 0.7 mm is the safer specification for high-alcohol mouthwash.

Why does sunscreen packaging matter so much?

UV filters attack some gaskets and inks, and the pack is squeezed with wet hands. A pump or disc-top with a silicone seal and a grip shape is the practical answer.

Are contact lens bottles special?

Yes. The nozzle has to keep the contents sterile, so the pack is usually HDPE or multi-layer with a one-way nozzle, filled in a controlled area.

What size is micellar water sold in?

200–400 ml. The size is set by shelf facing rather than formula, and the same shape usually runs in two sizes with one neck.
