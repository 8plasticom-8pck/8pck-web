Disc-Top Caps: 20/410, 24/410 and the Hinge That Fails

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 12 November 2026

A disc-top opens one-handed with a wet hand, which is why shampoo and body wash use it. The specification question is the orifice, the deck diameter and the hinge that snaps after a few hundred uses.

The same closure is sold as a disc-top cap, a disc cap, a disc top, a press-top and sometimes a flip-top, and the names are not interchangeable to a buyer. The disc-top is pressed on one side of the lid and hinges open around a living hinge; a flip-top has a separate lid on its own hinge and does not pivot on the deck.

For shampoo, body wash, hand wash, lotion and shower gel it is the default closure, because it opens with a thumb while the other hand holds the bottle. Its weakness is the same feature that makes it useful: an exposed hinge and a lid that traps water.

The Three Numbers That Decide the Cap

Orifice first: 3 mm for a lotion that should come out in a ribbon, 5–6 mm for a shampoo that has to empty fast. The orifice is moulded into the cap, so changing it means a different cap, not a different bottle.

Deck diameter second: 33–38 mm on a 24/410. That number decides whether the pack fits under a shelf lip and whether the cap survives the carton divider.

Height third: 25–30 mm, which is what the carton and the pallet see. A 5 mm taller cap across 200,000 units is a different pallet count.

Where It Fails: the Hinge, Then the Seal

The hinge is the first failure. A living hinge is a thin section of PP that flexes every time the cap is opened; at a few hundred cycles it cracks, and the lid comes away in the hand. Thicker hinges last longer and cost more material — that is the whole trade.

The second failure is the lid seal. A disc-top left open in a shower collects water on the deck; a thin product finds the gap between lid and deck on the second day and the bottle weeps into the wash bag. It is why we ask for a filled sample left on its side for 48 hours before approval, not an empty one.

The third is the thread: a 24/410 cap on a 24/410 neck from a different mould can be tight at the start and loose after two openings, because thread start angle and pitch differ by supplier. Send the bottle or the neck drawing, not just the words 24/410.

Disc-Top or Flip-Top: the One-Minute Test

Hold the bottle in one hand, the way a customer does in the shower, and open it. If the thumb has to find an edge, it is the wrong closure for the product. A disc-top pivots on the deck and is found blind; a flip-top needs the lid edge under the thumb.

Then look at the product. Oily or creamy products creep past a flip-top hinge and leave a ring of residue on the shoulder; thin liquids find the seam of a disc-top deck. Those two sentences cover most of the choice between them.

If the pack has to survive a bag, neither: a screw cap with a liner, or a valve cap, wins on leakage at the cost of one-handed use.

Necks: 20/410 and 24/410, and Nothing Else in Stock

A 20/410 disc-top suits 50–200 ml travel and toner sizes; a 24/410 suits 250–500 ml shampoo and body wash. Both are stock moulds — no tooling — and both are supplied fitted and leak-tested on the bottle.

If the brief asks for a disc-top on a 28/410 neck, the same bottle usually takes a trigger or a lotion pump instead. The neck is the constant; the closure is the variable per market.

What to Send in a Brief

Neck, orifice, product viscosity and quantity. Four answers are enough to quote, and the viscosity is the one buyers forget — it decides whether 3 mm or 6 mm is the right orifice, and it is the difference between a controlled pour and a splash.

Ordering Closures Without Ordering the Wrong Ones

A closure is only as good as the bottle it is tested on. Two suppliers can each be within tolerance and still leak as a pair, so the test has to be on the filled pack.

Send the Bottle, Not the Drawing

Dimensions do not capture thread start or the sealing land.

Send the Product

A gasket that passes with water can swell in a week with a solvent.

Agree the Sample

Keep one filled, approved sample as the reference for the production run.

Cost and Lead Time

Closures are quoted per 1,000 pcs and up, with a separate tooling cost only if the shape does not exist. Standard caps and pumps are stock items; anything moulded for you starts at 3,000–5,000 pcs.

Questions We Get on This

What is a disc-top cap?

A one-piece PP closure that pivots open on the deck and dispenses through a moulded orifice. It is the standard closure for shampoo, body wash and lotion.

Is a disc-top the same as a flip-top?

No. A disc-top pivots on the deck; a flip-top has a separate lid on its own living hinge. Buyers and suppliers use the names loosely, so specify with a photo or a sample.

What neck does a disc-top fit?

20/410 and 24/410 in our stock range. 18/410 exists for small bottles, 28/410 usually means a trigger or pump instead.

What orifice for shampoo?

5–6 mm. 3 mm suits lotion and cream; a thicker gel needs 6 mm or more.

What is the MOQ?

1,000 pcs per colour on existing moulds.

Can you test it with my formula?

Yes — send 200 ml of product and we test the closure fitted on the bottle, left on its side for 48 hours.

Do you supply closures separately?

Yes, but they are tested on the bottle before they ship.

What if my bottle is from another supplier?

Send a sample; we test the closure on your bottle.

Can you match a colour?

Yes — closure and bottle can be colour-matched from a Pantone reference.
