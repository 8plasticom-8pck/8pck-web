Dropper Bottle Sizes: What Each Volume Is Actually For

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 26 October 2026

From 2.5 ml sample vials to 100 ml oil bottles, the volume decides the neck, the pipette and whether the dose can be measured at all.

Dropper bottles are sold by volume, but they are specified by neck. The same 30 ml body can be moulded with an 18/410 neck for a bulb pipette or a 20/410 neck for a wider closure, and that single number decides which pipettes, caps and filling nozzles will fit the bottle for the rest of its life.

Below 10 ml the bottle stops being a pack and becomes a sample: the pipette is short, the dose is a few drops, and the cap is often a child-resistant closure. Above 50 ml the pipette becomes long enough to reach the bottom of the bottle, and the glass tube — not the bottle — is usually the most expensive part of the assembly.

The Neck Decides More Than the Volume

An 18/410 neck is the default for 2.5 to 50 ml. It takes the standard bulb pipette, the child-resistant closure used on CBD and essential oils, and most filling nozzles without an adapter. Moving to 20/410 buys a wider bore and a longer pipette, which matters above about 50 ml where the tip has to reach the last 10 ml of oil.

The mistake is to choose the bottle first and the closure second. A mould that only exists with an 18/410 neck will not take a 24/410 dosing pump, and cutting a new tool to change the neck costs more than choosing a different stock mould.

What Each Band Is Actually Used For

2.5 to 5 ml is sample and tester territory: beauty subscription boxes, hotel amenities, and the small vials that come with a full-size purchase. The pack is opened once or twice, so the pipette can be the cheapest bulb type.

10 to 30 ml is the serum and tincture band, and it is the volume that carries the highest value per millilitre. Here the pipette matters commercially: a push-button dropper costs 20–30 % more than a bulb but holds a fixed dose, which is what a serum sold on precision needs.

50 to 100 ml is oil: hair oil, body oil, massage oil, beard oil. The pipette is long, the glass is thicker, and the bottle is often the same body as a 100 ml lotion bottle with a different neck insert. At this size the customer usually refills, so the closure has to survive being opened weekly for a year.

Doses per Bottle — the Number Buyers Forget

A 30 ml serum with a standard pipette delivers roughly 0.06–0.08 ml per draw, so the bottle is good for 300–480 uses. A 100 ml oil at 0.06–0.10 ml per draw is 1,000–1,600 uses. Those numbers matter for two reasons: they set the label claim — a 30-day supply — and they tell you how much preservative and how much headspace the formula needs.

If the marketing copy says a bottle lasts a month, check the maths against the dose before the artwork goes to print. It is cheaper to change the pipette than the label.

Glass, PET or HDPE: the Choice That Changes the Shelf Life

PET is the default for 10–100 ml because it is clear, cheap and does not shatter in a bathroom. It is not a barrier: a citrus oil in PET will lose its top notes faster than in glass, and a formula with more than about 20 % alcohol can stress-crack the shoulder over months.

HDPE takes the same neck and is the right answer for anything opaque, acidic or sold in a hot climate — a vitamin C serum in clear PET is a formula problem, not a packaging problem. Glass remains the premium option for 30 ml serums and perfume; it is heavier to ship, roughly three times the freight per unit at the same volume, and it is the reason a 30 ml glass dropper costs more than a 100 ml PET bottle.

Sampling: What to Check Before You Approve a Size

Ask for a filled sample, not an empty bottle. The three failures at this size are the pipette not reaching the last few millilitres, the wiper flooding the glass tube so the dose doubles, and the cap not seating once the pipette collar is fitted. None of them shows up on a drawing.

Then weigh it. A 30 ml PET dropper with a glass pipette and cap runs 22–28 g; the same bottle with a plastic push-button dropper is 2–4 g lighter. At 20,000 units that difference is a tonne of freight, and it is invisible in a quote that only lists unit price.

Finally, check the label area. On a 30 ml bottle the usable print height between shoulder and base is about 45 mm, which fits a 40 mm label. A design built for a 50 mm label will not fit, and shrinking it loses the mandatory text.

Packing and Freight by Size

Small droppers are dense: a 30 ml bottle at 28 g gross is 34 kg per 1,000 pcs before cartons. Carton counts run from 500 pcs for 30 ml to 200 pcs for 100 ml, and the carton dimensions are usually set by the height of the assembled pipette rather than the bottle body.

This is why a 100 ml dropper quote can come in higher than a 250 ml lotion bottle: the pack is not heavier, but it is taller, and containers fill by volume, not weight.

Questions We Get on This

What is the most common dropper bottle size?

30 ml with an 18/410 neck. It is the default serum and tincture size in the US and EU.

Can a 30 ml bottle take a pump instead of a dropper?

Yes, if the neck is 18/410 the same body can take a lotion pump or a disc-top. The body does not change; the closure does.

What does 18/410 mean?

18 mm is the neck diameter to the outside of the thread, 410 is the thread profile. A pipette or cap labelled 18/410 fits any bottle with that finish.

Are 2.5 ml dropper bottles available with child-resistant caps?

Yes, on the 18/410 neck. It is the standard closure for essential oils and CBD in small volumes.

How many drops are in a 30 ml bottle?

Roughly 300–480 pipette draws at 0.06–0.08 ml each, depending on the pipette and the viscosity of the liquid.
