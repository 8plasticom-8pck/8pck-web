Sprayers: Fine Mist, Trigger and Perfume

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 26 October 2026

Three sprayers, three jobs: a fine mist for a face, a trigger for a floor, a crimp or screw sprayer for a fragrance. The output and the spring decide the pack.

A sprayer is judged by the first spray: too coarse and it wets the face, too fine and it takes twenty pumps to cover a worktop. That first impression is set by output per stroke and by the nozzle orifice, not by the bottle.

The second judgement comes months later, when the spring has met the chemistry. Ammonia, bleach and salt solutions destroy a standard steel spring, and the sprayer stops working while the bottle is still half full.

Fine Mist: the Orifice Is the Product

A face mist needs 0.05–0.15 ml per stroke and a nozzle that produces a soft cone. The same pump body with a different orifice insert produces a jet that wets the face, which is why orifice choice is a specification and not an afterthought.

Salt and preservative in the formula are what clog a fine mist. A 0.1 mm orifice with a saline formula will crust at the tip within weeks; the fix is a slightly larger orifice and a formulation check, not a different brand of sprayer.

Trigger: Output and Reach

Household triggers deliver 0.9–1.6 ml per stroke, and the difference between the low and high end is the difference between a fine mist over a worktop and a soaking. For a degreaser the higher output is correct; for a sanitiser it wastes product.

The spring is the failure point in ammonia and bleach. A passivated spring or a plastic-compatible alloy costs more and lasts the life of the pack; a standard steel spring fails within months in a bathroom.

Perfume Sprayers: Crimp or Screw

A crimp sprayer is sealed onto a glass bottle with a crimping tool and cannot be refilled; it is the standard for retail fragrance because it holds vapour. A screw sprayer is used for small batches and samples, and it loses a little top note through the thread over months.

The crimp is a skill: an under-crimped collar leaks, an over-crimped one cracks the glass neck. For any fragrance order, the crimping is done at the filler, not at the bottle supplier, and the two have to agree on the neck specification.

Dip Tube, Again

Every sprayer has a dip tube cut to the bottle. A tube that is too long curls and the sprayer sucks air; too short and the last part of the bottle cannot be sprayed. The length is set by bottle height minus the sprayer's collar depth, and it is the most common assembly error in spray packs.

For 500 ml and larger bottles, the tube also needs a slight angle or a weighted end to reach the corner when the bottle is tilted. That is a one-cent part that removes a frequent complaint.

Output, Strokes and the Label Claim

A 500 ml trigger at 1.0 ml is about 500 strokes; a 100 ml fine mist at 0.1 ml is about 1,000. Those numbers are what a 'lasts X uses' claim rests on, and they are set by the sprayer, not the bottle.

If the claim is important, specify the output tolerance with the sprayer supplier. A 20 % drift in output is normal across a large production run, and it is enough to make a label claim untrue at the end of the batch.

Questions We Get on This

What output should a face mist have?

0.05–0.15 ml per stroke with a soft cone nozzle. Anything above that wets the face instead of misting.

Why does my trigger stop working?

Usually spring corrosion in ammonia or bleach, or a worn piston seal. Specify a passivated spring for chemical products.

Crimp or screw for perfume?

Crimp for retail fragrance because it holds vapour; screw for samples and small batches, accepting slight top-note loss.

Can I use the same bottle for a mist and a trigger?

Only if the neck matches. Fine mist is usually 18–24 mm, trigger is 28 mm, so the bottle differs.

How many sprays in a 100 ml mist bottle?

About 700–1,000 at 0.1–0.15 ml per stroke, depending on the orifice and the formula.
