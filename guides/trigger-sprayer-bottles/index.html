Trigger Sprayer Bottles: the Head Fails, Not the Bottle

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 26 October 2026

In a trigger spray pack the bottle is commodity and the trigger is the product. Output per stroke, neck standard and the spring decide whether it lasts a year or a month.

A 500 ml trigger spray is one of the cheapest packs in the catalogue and one of the most returned. The bottle almost never fails: the trigger head, the spring or the nozzle does, and the customer reports all three as 'the spray broke'.

That is why a trigger pack should be specified from the head backwards. Choose the output and the chemistry first, then the neck, then the bottle.

28/410 Or 28/400 — a 0.4 mm Decision

Both are 28 mm necks; the difference is the thread profile. 28/410 is the European standard and the default for most trigger heads sold worldwide. 28/400 is common on US domestic triggers and will screw onto a 28/410 bottle, but the thread engagement is shallower, so it strips earlier under torque and leaks sooner at the gasket.

If the pack ships to the US with a locally sourced trigger, specify 28/400 on the bottle and accept that the same bottle will not take a European head. If one bottle has to serve both markets, 28/410 is the safer single standard.

Output per Stroke Is a Specification, Not a Detail

Household multi-surface triggers deliver about 1 ml per stroke, so a 500 ml bottle is roughly 500 strokes. Fine mist heads deliver 0.2 ml, which is right for sanitiser and glass but wrong for a degreaser where the user expects a wet hit.

Two numbers follow from the output: the number of strokes per bottle (which drives the 'lasts X uses' claim) and the dose per square metre, which is what a chemical brand actually sells. Get the output wrong and the product either floods the surface or takes twenty pumps to cover a worktop.

What Actually Breaks, in Order

First the spring: a steel spring in a bleach or acid formula corrodes within months unless it is passivated or replaced with a plastic-compatible alloy. Second the nozzle: hard water and viscous products clog a fine mist orifice. Third the piston seal: it wears and the trigger loses prime, which users describe as 'it stopped spraying'.

The cheapest way to avoid all three is to test the head with the actual chemistry for 72 hours before the order, not to test it with water. A trigger that works with water and fails with bleach is the most common packaging complaint in household chemicals.

Bottle Choice: PET or HDPE

HDPE is the default because most household chemicals are opaque and the bottle is squeezed in use. PET is used where clarity sells — a coloured cleaner, a sanitiser sold on shelf — but PET stress-cracks under some solvents and is a poor choice for anything with a high aromatic content.

For PCR content, HDPE is easier: recycled HDPE is widely available in food and non-food grades, while recycled PET for chemical packs needs a barrier discussion. A 30–50 % PCR HDPE trigger bottle is now routine.

The Dip Tube, and Why Length Is a Spec

A trigger's dip tube has to reach the bottom corner of the bottle, and be cut to length for that bottle. Too short and the last 15 % of product cannot be sprayed; too long and it curls and the trigger sucks air.

This is why a trigger head and a bottle are a pair. Buying heads from one supplier and bottles from another is fine, but the tube length has to be specified for the bottle, and the sample has to be tested assembled — not as two components.

Decoration and What It Costs on a Trigger Pack

Trigger bottles are usually labelled rather than sleeved, because the trigger and collar interfere with a sleeve. A BOPP label survives a wet bathroom and a bleach wipe; a paper label does not.

Silkscreen directly on HDPE is possible but it is a two-pass job on a curved surface, and the registration drifts. For runs under about 10,000 a printed label is cheaper and looks better; above that, screen printing starts to win on unit cost.

What a Trigger Pack Costs to Convert, Roughly

The bottle is the cheap part: a 500 ml HDPE bottle is a small fraction of the assembled pack. The head carries most of the cost, and the spread between a basic household trigger and a fine mist head is wide enough to move the whole pack price by 20–30 %.

That is the opposite of the bottle-first thinking most buyers arrive with. On a trigger pack, specifying the bottle first and finding the head afterwards is how the 28/400 versus 28/410 problem happens.

Packing and the Trigger-on or Trigger-Off Decision

Triggers ship either fitted or loose. Fitted is faster at the filling line and better for retail, but it adds height to every carton and can be broken in transit. Loose heads pack denser and cost less to ship, at the cost of an assembly step at the filler.

The threshold is roughly 20,000 units: below it, loose heads and a manual assembly jig are cheaper; above it, a fitted line pays for itself. Either way, the carton count per pallet changes, and that is where the freight difference appears.

Questions We Get on This

What neck does a trigger sprayer use?

28/410 is the standard worldwide. 28/400 appears on many US domestic triggers and fits the same bottle but with shallower thread engagement.

How many sprays in a 500 ml bottle?

About 450–550 strokes at 0.9–1.1 ml per stroke for a standard household trigger. A fine mist head at 0.2 ml gives roughly 2,500.

Why does my trigger stop spraying?

Usually the piston seal has worn or the nozzle is clogged, not the bottle. Test the head with the actual chemical, not water, before ordering.

Can a trigger bottle hold bleach?

Yes, in HDPE with a corrosion-resistant spring. A standard steel spring will fail within months in hypochlorite.

Are trigger heads recyclable?

The head is mixed material — PP body, steel spring, LDPE tube — so it is usually not recyclable in kerbside streams. The bottle is; the head is the loss.
