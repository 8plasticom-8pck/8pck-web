Bottle Caps and Closures: Six Types, One Neck

By Joe Joo · 8PCK · Shantou · Updated 12 November 2026

Six closure types fit the same bottles and fail in six different ways. The product decides which one; the failure mode tells you whether you chose wrong.

A cap is not a commodity. Six closure types are used on plastic bottles, they cost between a few cents and several times that, and each one fails in a specific, predictable way. Choosing the wrong one does not show up in a quotation; it shows up as a leak in a container or a cracked hinge in a customer's hand.

The neck is the constant. A 24/410 bottle can take a disc-top, a flip-top, a screw cap, a lotion pump or a trigger, so the neck is specified once and the closure is chosen per market, per product and per channel.

Six Types, One Neck: Where to Read Next

Each closure has a page of its own, because the decision inside each one is different. Start here, then read the one that matches the product.

Disc-top — orifice, deck diameter and a hinge that cracks at a few hundred cycles. Disc-top caps.

Flip-top — the cheapest one-handed closure, and the one that creeps open with oily product. Flip-top caps.

Screw cap and liner — the liner seals, not the cap; the wrong liner leaks after a lorry ride. Screw caps.

Pumps — dose per stroke depends on the pack, and the dip tube has to reach the last 10 ml. Lotion pumps.

Child-resistant — a legal requirement, not an upgrade, and it often forces a specific neck thread. Child-resistant caps.

Valve caps — travel and hotel, and the valve is what leaks in a bag. Valve caps.

The Liner Does the Sealing, Not the Cap

Every screw cap holds a liner: EPE foam, a PE disc or an induction foil. The cap only keeps the liner in compression. Swap the liner without telling the buyer and the leak rate changes while the cap looks identical.

Two failures follow from the wrong liner. Too hard, and it seals at the factory but leaks after a lorry ride as the plastic relaxes. Too soft, and it deforms into the thread so the cap backs off. Both are found by testing the assembled pack, not the parts.

Child-Resistant: a Legal Requirement, Not an Upgrade

A CR closure needs two motions: squeeze and turn, or push down and turn. It costs more than a standard cap and slows the filling line, because each cap has to be oriented and torqued rather than pressed on.

The packaging consequence is at the neck: a CR cap often needs a specific thread form and a collar, so a bottle specified for a screw cap cannot simply be upgraded later without a new tool.

Measuring Caps and the Dose Problem

Fabric softener and detergent use a measuring cap, and the dose lines are the part that fails in use: moulded-in graduations are hard to read in a dim utility room, and printed ones wear off.

The practical fix is a cap with a raised line at the standard dose rather than a full scale, and a transparent or white cap so the meniscus is visible. A small design decision that removes a common complaint.

Valve Caps and Travel

A valve cap lets a shower gel or shampoo dispense without opening, which is why hotels and gyms use them. The failure is the valve itself: a bottle squeezed inside a wash bag will push product past a cheap silicone valve.

For travel sizes the safer combination is a valve cap plus a tight secondary closure, or simply a flip-top. A valve that leaks once in a suitcase loses the customer permanently.

Tamper Evidence, Cheapest to Best

Three options: an induction seal inside the cap, a drop-ring that breaks on first opening, and a shrink band. The induction seal is the strongest and is standard for food and chemicals; the drop-ring is cheaper and common on personal care; the shrink band is the cheapest and the most often removed before it reaches a shelf.

For export to the EU and US, a drop-ring plus an induction seal is the usual combination. A shrink band alone is a weak claim, and retailers increasingly reject it.

Questions We Get on This

What is the most common bottle cap?

24/410 in PP. It is the default neck for 250–500 ml personal care, and the same neck takes a disc-top, flip-top, screw cap or lotion pump.

Can I change the cap without changing the bottle?

Yes, if the neck stays the same. A 24/410 bottle takes disc-top, flip-top, screw or pump closures.

Which cap leaks least in transit?

A screw cap with an induction seal. Pumps and disc-tops are tested on the bottle, but a sealed screw cap is designed to survive a container.

Do child-resistant caps need a special bottle?

Often yes. CR closures usually need a specific thread and collar, so the bottle has to be specified with the CR cap from the start.

What is 24/410?

24 mm neck diameter, 410 thread profile — the most common personal care standard worldwide.

Is a disc-top the same as a flip-top?

No. A disc-top pivots on the deck; a flip-top has a separate lid on a living hinge. The disc-top has its own page here.
